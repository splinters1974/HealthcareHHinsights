Moving away from gas CHP

Gas CHP was a sound carbon choice for many hospitals ten years ago. As the grid has cleaned up, the sums have changed. Here is why, what to do with the engines you have, and the electrical surprise waiting when one stops.

By Paul Johnston, Managing Director, Kinetic Energy Consulting · Last updated 18 November 2026

Gas combined heat and power (CHP) saves carbon only when the grid electricity it replaces is more carbon-intensive than burning the extra gas. With the UK grid factor at about 0.13 kg CO₂e per kWh for 2026 reporting, a typical gas CHP engine now emits more than buying electricity from the grid and making the heat in a boiler.1 It can still cut costs when gas is cheap relative to electricity, and it adds resilience, but it does not lead to net zero. When a CHP engine stops, the site's grid import rises by its full electrical output, which is often the first capacity problem a trust meets.

Why the carbon case has changed

Here is a simple comparison, per kWh of gas burned in an engine with typical efficiencies. It is illustrative: your engine's own efficiencies, and how much of its heat you use, will change the answer.

Assumption or resultValue
Natural gas, kg CO₂e per kWh (gross calorific value), 2026 factors0.182
Grid electricity, kg CO₂e per kWh, 2026 factors0.131
CHP electrical efficiency / heat efficiency (assumed)35% / 45%
Boiler efficiency for the alternative (assumed)90%
CHP: 1 kWh of gas gives 0.35 kWh of electricity and 0.45 kWh of heat0.182 kg
Grid plus boiler: 0.35 kWh from the grid, 0.5 kWh of gas for the heat0.137 kg
Heat pump instead of boiler: 0.35 kWh from the grid, plus 0.18 kWh to make 0.45 kWh of heat at an efficiency of 2.50.069 kg

On these assumptions, the CHP route emits about a third more than grid electricity and a boiler, and more than twice as much as grid electricity and a heat pump. Factors are from the UK government's 2026 conversion factors.1 With the 2025 grid factor of 0.177, the CHP route was still about a fifth worse. Ten years ago, when the grid factor was far higher, CHP came out ahead, which is why so many were installed.

Some engineers argue that CHP displaces the gas power stations at the margin of the grid rather than the average mix, which flatters CHP. That debate is real, but NHS carbon reporting uses the average, location-based factors, so that is what your Green Plan figures will show.

What the policies say

NHS Wales has the clearest position: no further fossil-fuelled CHP will be installed, existing CHP should be maintained at maximum efficiency until life expired, decommissioning is preferred over major refurbishment such as an engine replacement, and all fossil CHP is to be decommissioned by 2035. Renewable CHP is still supported.2 In England, the 2040 net zero target for direct emissions leaves no room for gas CHP in the long run, because the gas it burns is a direct emission.5

Options for the engine you have

  1. Run it well to the end of its life. If it is reliable and its heat is fully used, keep it running efficiently while the replacement is planned.
  2. Do not re-engine by default. A major overhaul can lock in another decade of gas. Price the alternatives at the same time.
  3. Change how it runs. Running only when electricity prices are high, and heat is needed, can keep the cost benefit while cutting run hours.
  4. Plan its replacement. Heat pumps for the base heat load, heat recovery from cooling, and boilers for peaks, as at Bridlington Hospital, where the CHP was decommissioned and a 600 kW air source heat pump system installed.3
  5. Think about resilience separately. CHP is sometimes counted on for resilience. Standby generators, batteries and supply arrangements may do that job better once the engine goes.

The capacity surprise

While a CHP engine runs, the site's main meter only records what the CHP does not supply. So the half-hourly data you get from your supplier understates the site's real electrical demand by the engine's output. When the engine is off for maintenance, trips, or is removed, grid import jumps by that amount. If a 1 MW engine stops, the site needs about 1 MW more from the grid, roughly 1,050 kVA at a power factor of 0.95.

To see this in your own data:

  • Load the main import meter into the analyser and look at the heatmap. Engine outages show as bands of higher import.
  • If you have the CHP generation meter's data, load it as a separate meter and add the two together to see the site's true demand.
  • Run the headroom check against the agreed supply capacity with the engine's output as a test load. If the answer is negative, removing the CHP needs a connection upgrade or a matching cut in demand.

Welsh estates already report CHP units as part of their EFPMS return, so the data on engine sizes and run hours often exists.4

Questions people ask

Does gas CHP still save carbon?

In most cases, no, when measured with the UK government's average grid factor. At 2026 factors a typical gas engine emits about a third more than buying grid electricity and making the heat in a boiler. It can still save money, depending on gas and electricity prices.

Should we replace our CHP engine when it wears out?

Not by default. Price a heat pump and heat recovery scheme at the same time. In Wales, policy prefers decommissioning to major refurbishment and plans to end fossil CHP by 2035.

Why does grid import rise when CHP stops?

The main meter only sees what the CHP does not supply. When the engine stops, the site draws its full output from the grid instead, so peaks rise and headroom shrinks.
