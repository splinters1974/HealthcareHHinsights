Base load on healthcare sites

On a 24-hour hospital, the load that never switches off is usually the biggest part of the electricity bill. It's also the part most often left unexamined.

By Paul Johnston, Managing Director, Kinetic Energy Consulting · Last updated 22 November 2026

Base load is the level of electricity a site never drops below, day or night. On a hospital it covers wards, ventilation, imaging, fridges, IT and medical gas plant, and it is often the largest part of the bill. In our made-up community hospital, it's 63%. Each kilowatt running all year is 8,766 kWh, or about £2,190 at 25p/kWh, so even small cuts add up.

How the analyser measures it

The analyser converts every valid half-hourly reading to kW and takes the level demand stays above 95% of the time (the 5th percentile). It doesn't use the single lowest reading, because a generator test, a meter fault or a power cut can set that. On a hospital, the monthly generator test often sets the lowest half hour of the year, which is why the minimum is shown alongside the base load but not used for it.

Two more views matter on a 24-hour site:

  • Base load by month. A step up that doesn't follow the weather means something started running and didn't stop. Check this chart on every hospital.
  • The low-activity window. The 24/7 preset uses 01:00 to 05:00, when wards are quiet and clinics, theatres and kitchens are shut. Demand in that window is as close to a hospital's "closed" state as you'll get.

Theatre ventilation: the biggest single base load saving

HTM 03-01 describes switching off or setting back theatre ventilation when it isn't needed as the most energy-efficient policy. NHS England's July 2026 estates technical bulletin estimates the saving at 132 to 286 MWh per theatre each year, across about 3,300 theatres in England.1 The bulletin makes the trust's Ventilation Safety Group the starting point, and sets conditions: pressure cascades checked, temperatures kept between 16°C and 25°C while set back, and enough time to return to full flow before use (25 to 30 minutes after a long shutdown).1

In half-hourly data, a working setback shows as lower nights and weekends on the theatre block's meter, or on the main meter if theatres are a large share of the site. When a setback stops working, base load steps up and stays up, as it does in our worked example.

How to cut it safely

  1. Walk the site between 01:00 and 05:00 with the night site manager or porters. Note what's on in empty offices, outpatients, restaurants and plant rooms.
  2. Sort it into three lists: must run (clinical, safety, fridges, medical gas), could run less (pumps, fans, lighting, IT cooling), and shouldn't be on.
  3. Fix the "shouldn't be on" list first. BMS schedules, timers and plant left in hand cost almost nothing to put right.
  4. Then the "could run less" list: theatre setback, variable speed drives on pumps and fans, LED and sensors in 24-hour corridors.
  5. Check base load by month afterwards. It should step down and stay down.

Never switch off clinical equipment, fridges, medical gas plant, fire and security systems or water safety controls to save energy.

Base load and solar

A hospital's base load runs through every sunny afternoon, including weekends, so it soaks up solar output. That's why 24-hour sites usually use nearly all of what a well-sized system generates. The analyser's Solar tab estimates the share from your own readings and passes it to the solar calculator. See sizing solar from half-hourly data.

Working out the saving

Multiply the kilowatts you remove by the hours they would have run and by your unit rate. A load running all year is 8,766 hours (the average year, allowing for leap years), so 1 kW is about £2,190 a year at 25p/kWh. A load that only runs at night and weekends, like a failed theatre setback, runs for about 5,700 hours a year.

Sources

  1. NHS England: NHS Estates Technical Bulletin 2026/1, generating energy efficiency savings through controlling theatre ventilation (July 2026)

Questions people ask

What share of a hospital's electricity is base load?

It varies by site, so measure your own. On a 24-hour hospital it is commonly more than half. In our made-up community hospital it is 63%.

Why not use the lowest half hour as the base load?

Because one generator test, meter fault or power cut can set it. The analyser uses the 5th percentile, which those short events barely move.

How much does 1 kW of base load cost a year?

About 8,766 kWh, which is roughly £2,190 at 25p/kWh. Multiply 8,766 by your own unit rate.

Is theatre ventilation setback safe?

NHS England guidance supports it when theatres are unused, under conditions on pressure, temperature and time to return to full flow, and with the Ventilation Safety Group overseeing it.
